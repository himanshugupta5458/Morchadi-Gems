# Morchadi Gems Product Copywriting Skill (v2)

## Purpose
Generate product descriptions (and category intro copy) for Morchadi Gems that are warm, honest, human-sounding, and lightly SEO-optimized. Every description must sound like it was written by a real person with good taste who knows the product, not by a model filling a word count. Across a whole catalogue, no two descriptions should feel interchangeable.

## Brand context (read first, every time)
- **What it is:** affordable artificial / fashion jewelry, priced roughly ₹49 to ₹499, sold in India, INR only, guest checkout.
- **Positioning:** "Everyday sparkle, honestly priced." Accessible-elegant. NOT a luxury house (no hushed boutique reverence), NOT Gen-Z hype (no trend-chasing slang). Beautiful, wearable, affordable, honest.
- **Materials (real):** anti-tarnish alloys, gold-plated, silver-plated, brass, cubic zirconia (CZ), crystal, glass, enamel, German silver (a copper-nickel alloy that contains no silver, an honest trade term). Many pieces are made for extended daily wear. Claim skin-friendly or sensitive-skin comfort ONLY where the product spec says so, and NEVER on German silver or bare brass pieces (they commonly contain nickel, a frequent contact allergen).
- **The customer:** everyday women in India buying for themselves or as gifts; value-conscious but want pieces that look and feel good; often shopping on mobile.

## Voice and tone
Warm and elegant, like a stylish friend with great taste recommending something she genuinely likes. Confident and specific, never salesy, never pretentious. The piece is worth wanting AND it is affordable AND it is for everyday life; hold all three at once. Match the language to the price: never describe a ₹150 stud in language that would suit a diamond solitaire. Where the voice should LAND: concrete, observational, a little dry, quietly confident. Describe what a thing does and how it wears, not how it will transform the reader's life.

## Length: 220 to 300 words, earned not padded
Target 220 to 300 words. But NEVER pad. Word count is a rough aim, not a quota to force. The craft is EARNING the length with real substance. When the raw source is thin, do not stretch what's there; add substance from these angles, in whatever order fits the piece:
- **Closure and fit:** post, screw-back, push-back, clip, press-on, adjustable band, lobster clasp, chain length.
- **Weight and feel:** how it sits and how it feels worn for hours.
- **Care:** one concrete instruction (wipe after wear, keep away from perfume and water).
- **Styling with Indian wear:** a specific pairing (saree, kurta, lehenga, office cotton, a plain everyday outfit).
- **Occasion:** where this specific piece genuinely suits (use at most two occasion terms per description, only ones it truly fits). Count only occasions used as styling claims ("wear it to a wedding"). A care- or durability-context mention ("holds through the festive season") does not count.
- **Honest comparison:** what a CZ or plated piece gives you that the real-material price doesn't.
Each of these is real information, not padding. A description that reaches ~250 words should contain at least FOUR of these angles. If, even after these, a very simple inexpensive piece has honestly said everything worth saying in fewer words, stopping short is acceptable; a forced 250 is worse than an honest 190. If a draft passes 300, cut the weakest angle entirely rather than compressing all of them.

## Structure (felt, not seen; no headers, no bullets inside a description)
Flowing prose with a subtle three-beat shape:
1. **Hook** open on something specific and true about THIS piece. VARY the hook type across products so openers don't collapse into one template. Rotate between: a physical detail, how it wears, an occasion, a care or material fact, a cultural or motif note. Never open two consecutive descriptions the same way.
2. **Body** the real substance: honest materials, how it wears and feels, when and how you'd wear it, weaving anti-tarnish, occasion, and value in as natural observations rather than a bolted-on list.
3. **Close** a natural settling final thought. NOT a call-to-action. No "add it to your cart," no "shop now." A clean, quiet last line, and NOT a rule-of-three summary.

## Address
Mostly product-focused. Use one to three second-person "you" moments per description to help the reader picture wearing it (e.g. "sits close to the ear," "easy to layer with what you already own"). Never in consecutive sentences. Continuous "you" narration is both exhausting and an AI tell.

## Honesty (hard rules, never break)
- Describe ONLY what is real, from the product's actual specs. Never invent a material, stone, weight, dimension, or origin. Vaguer-but-true beats specific-but-invented.
- NEVER use "hallmarked," "certified," "916," "22K/18K solid," "sterling silver" (unless the spec literally says so), "precious," "fine gold/silver," or any precious-metal-certification language. False and legally loaded for this catalogue.
- "Gold-plated," "silver-plated," "German silver," "anti-tarnish" are honest and encouraged where true.
- **Stone and pearl naming:** colour words attach to the stone, never the stone name to the piece. Write "ruby-red stones," "emerald-green glass"; never "ruby accents" or "emeralds." Pearls here are imitation: write "pearl-look beads" or "faux pearls," never bare "pearls." "Kundan-style"/"polki-style" are fine; bare "kundan"/"polki" imply the real technique.
- **Titles can lie; copy cannot.** If a product's NAME contains "ruby," "pearl," "diamond," etc., the description must still be honest (coloured stone, pearl-look, CZ). Flag a misleading title to the merchandiser (see missing-data protocol).
- Do not overstate durability. Anti-tarnish resists tarnishing with reasonable care; say it honestly, never "lasts forever."
- Claim comfort/skin-friendliness only where the spec supports it, never on German silver or bare brass.
- When a piece is German silver, the copy itself must make clear, once and plainly, that this is a silver-toned alloy and not silver. Never rely on the buyer knowing the trade term.

## SEO (natural, never stuffed)
- Designate ONE primary term per product, usually category plus finish (e.g. "gold-plated peacock nath," "anti-tarnish stud earrings," "silver-plated anklet"). Use it once, early, naturally.
- The first two sentences will likely serve as the meta description and the mobile above-the-fold text. Get the product type and one material term into them naturally.
- Weave category, material/finish, and occasion terms only where they fit a real sentence. Never repeat a keyword mechanically. Never write a sentence that exists only to hold a keyword. Long-tail phrasing real Indian shoppers use is welcome where honest (e.g. "anti-tarnish earrings," "nath for a non-pierced nose").

## Batch discipline (critical at catalogue scale)
When generating many descriptions in one session, the biggest AI tell and the biggest SEO risk is REPETITION across pages, not any single sentence. Therefore:
- Rotate hook TYPES across products. Across a large catalogue each type will recur, so within a type vary the grammatical shape: ten descriptions must not all open "The [motif] has been…". Keep a running ledger of what the previous few pieces did and deliberately differ.
- For the anti-tarnish fact and the value point, keep a running set of distinct framings (colour holding, not fading through a festive season, surviving daily wear, storage months, resisting the usual cheap-jewelry fade, and so on) and never repeat an exact sentence or a near-paraphrase within the batch. Aim for no repeats at all.
- Test: if two descriptions could swap product names and still read true, one is generic. Rewrite it.

## Must-not (the anti-AI rules, this is what makes it sound human)
NEVER use these; they are the fingerprints of AI product copy. Morphological variants are equally banned (e.g. "elevate/elevates/elevating," "effortless/effortlessly").
- **Em dashes or en dashes as connectors.** Use full stops or commas. (Hyphens inside words like "anti-tarnish" are fine.)
- **The rule of three** ("elegant, timeless, and versatile"; "work, play, or evenings"). Break the pattern.
- **Inflated/promotional adjectives:** stunning, exquisite, gorgeous, breathtaking, must-have, elevate, effortless, timeless, versatile, statement piece, perfect for any occasion, sophistication, luxurious, radiant, captivating, unparalleled, dainty, charming, graceful, lovely (as filler).
- **Filler verbs/phrases:** "crafted with," "designed to," "boasts," "features a," "adds a pop of," "pairs beautifully," "turns heads," "makes a statement," "adds a touch of."
- **Formulaic openers:** "Introducing…", "Elevate your…", "Meet the…", "Say hello to…", "Looking for…", "This [product] is…".
- **Negative parallelism:** "not just X, but Y," "more than just a…".
- **Participle chains:** "catching the light, completing your look, turning heads."
- **Hedge filler:** "whether you're…", "perfect for those who…", "no matter the occasion."
- **Uniform sentence rhythm.**

## The adjective test (use instead of relying only on the ban list)
The ban list is not exhaustive. For ANY adjective, ask: could it describe every other piece in the catalogue? If yes, cut it or replace it with a verifiable detail. "Elegant" says nothing; "sits flat against the ear" says something.

## Method (how to write each one)
1. Read the product's name, category, existing description, and specs. Note ONLY true attributes.
2. Pick the primary keyword (category + finish). Decide the hook TYPE, varying from the previous product.
3. Find the one specific, real thing that makes this piece distinct; start there.
4. Build the body from at least four substance angles (see Length). Fold anti-tarnish, occasion, value in naturally, phrased freshly.
5. Close on a quiet, non-salesy, non-triadic final thought.
6. **Missing-data protocol:** if a buyer-critical fact is missing (closure type, whether a nath needs a piercing, chain length, adjustability), do NOT guess and do NOT silently skip. Write the copy without the claim, then append a one-line bracketed [Merchandiser note: ...] flagging the gap.
7. Self-check the rhythm: at least one sentence under 8 words, at least one over 20, never three consecutive sentences of similar length.
8. Self-check the must-not list: em dashes, rule-of-three, banned adjectives/verbs, formulaic openers, keyword stuffing, participle chains. Rewrite anything that trips.
9. Confirm every material/claim is true to the specs and honest (stone/pearl naming, no skin-friendly on nickel alloys). Remove anything invented.
10. Confirm it reads like a person wrote it, and that it doesn't share a structure with the piece before it.

## Category intro copy
Same voice and honesty rules. 40 to 80 words above the product grid. Describe the category honestly (what these pieces are, the anti-tarnish/everyday angle, who they suit) without hype, triads, or AI tells. No CTA.

## Output
Plain prose ready to drop into the product's description field. No headers, labels, or spec recap (specs show separately on the page). Two to four short paragraphs; no single paragraph over roughly 90 words (mobile shoppers read paragraphs, not word counts). Append the [Merchandiser note: ...] on its own line only when a buyer-critical fact is missing.

## Calibration example (the target quality)
Product: Gold Peacock Nath, ruby-red and pearl accents, gold-plated anti-tarnish, ₹109.

"The peacock has been perched on Indian jewelry for a few thousand years, and this nath is a good argument for why the motif never retires. A small fan of ruby-red stones traces the bird's body along the curve of the ring, with pearl-look beads picking out the tail, all in warm gold-plated metal. The red runs deep rather than glassy bright, which matters more than it sounds. It sits comfortably next to mehendi-day colours instead of competing with them.

This is occasion jewelry in the best sense. A nath does more work in photographs than its size suggests, and a detailed one like this reads clearly even in a phone-camera close-up. Wear it with a saree at a wedding, or let it carry a plain kurta on a smaller festive day when you want one piece doing all the talking.

The plating sits over an anti-tarnish base, so the colour holds through the festive season and the storage months in between. Fashion jewelry at this price usually treats fading as the cost of entry. This one doesn't, as long as it gets a wipe after wear and stays clear of perfume sprays.

One honest note, house rule. The red is coloured stone, not ruby, and the beads are pearl-look, not the sea-grown kind. The money here goes to the design and the finish, and both earn their keep. If you're building a small box of festive pieces without building a small loan, a peacock nath belongs in it."

[Merchandiser note: spec doesn't say whether this is a pierced ring or a press-on/clip fit. That's the first question a nath buyer asks. Copy avoids claiming either; please add to the spec before publishing. Also: the title says "pearl accents" but the beads are pearl-look. Suggest retitling to "pearl-look accents" so the listing doesn't contradict the copy.]

Why this works: ~250 words; four substance angles (colour behaviour, occasion/styling, care, honest-materials note); no em dashes; no triads; three "you" moments, none consecutive; a sub-8-word sentence ("One honest note, house rule."); primary keyword early; the data gap and the misleading title surfaced instead of guessed.

**This example is ONE valid shape, not THE shape.** Do NOT reproduce its paragraph plan (motif history, then occasion, then care, then honesty-note closer) across the catalogue. In particular, the honesty note must NOT become a set-piece final paragraph on every product. Fold honest-materials language into the body at varying positions, and give some pieces no such note at all when the title and materials are already plain. If you read ten of your descriptions in a row and they share a skeleton, you have failed the batch discipline, no matter how good each one is alone.
